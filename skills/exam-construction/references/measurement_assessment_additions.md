# Measurement-and-Assessment Additions for Exam Construction

Use these principles when the user wants a rigorous classroom test, final exam, item bank, rubric, or post-exam review.

## 1. Assessment language and inference

- A test is a standardized procedure for sampling, scoring, and evaluating behavior or performance.
- Measurement assigns numbers according to rules; assessment is the broader process of collecting information to support inferences and decisions.
- Treat every exam score as an imperfect sample of achievement, not as the full construct.
- State the intended inference before building the exam: mastery, relative standing, diagnostic feedback, instructional decision, or summative grading.

## 2. Score interpretation

Distinguish these interpretations explicitly:

- Norm-referenced interpretation: how a student performed relative to other students.
- Criterion-referenced interpretation: what a student can do relative to defined objectives or mastery criteria.
- Qualitative interpretation: descriptive labels, performance levels, or narrative feedback.

For classroom finals, default to criterion-referenced interpretation unless the user explicitly asks for relative grading.

## 3. Educational objectives and blueprinting

Before writing items:

1. Define objectives with appropriate scope.
2. Classify objectives by cognitive, affective, or psychomotor domain when relevant.
3. Convert broad objectives into assessable outcomes.
4. Construct a table of specifications / test blueprint.
5. Use the blueprint to control content coverage, cognitive level, item type, and score weight.

## 4. Reliability safeguards

Reliability concerns score consistency. For classroom exams, improve reliability by:

- Sampling enough items across the domain.
- Avoiding ambiguous wording and avoidable reading barriers.
- Using clear scoring keys and analytic rubrics.
- Using multiple items for important constructs rather than relying on a single question.
- Training or calibrating raters when constructed-response or performance tasks are used.
- Reporting the exam as a draft unless empirical reliability evidence has been obtained.

When response data are available, estimate internal consistency for objective items only when the exam is sufficiently homogeneous and sample size is adequate. Interpret reliability cautiously for small classes.

## 5. Validity evidence safeguards

Do not say an exam is “valid” in general. Specify validity evidence for the intended interpretation and use.

Consider five sources of evidence:

- Content evidence: representativeness of objectives and course content.
- Relations to other variables: association with relevant criteria or prior achievement when available.
- Internal structure: whether items behave consistently with intended dimensions when data are sufficient.
- Response processes: whether students are using the intended thinking process rather than test-taking tricks.
- Consequences: intended and unintended consequences of the assessment.

For teacher-made exams, content evidence and response-process review are usually the most feasible pre-administration evidence.

## 6. Item analysis after administration

When response data are provided, compute or request:

- Item difficulty index: proportion correct.
- Item discrimination: high-low group difference or corrected item-total correlation.
- Distractor analysis: frequency with which each distractor was selected, especially by high- vs low-performing students.
- Blank / nonresponse rate.
- Qualitative item review: ambiguity, miskey, mismatch with instruction, or construct-irrelevant difficulty.

Recommended decisions:

- Retain: acceptable difficulty and positive discrimination.
- Revise: plausible construct issue, weak discrimination, or nonfunctioning distractors.
- Discard or rescore: miskey, multiple defensible answers, or content not taught.
- Discuss in class: widespread misconception reflected by a functioning distractor.

## 7. Selected-response item extensions

Use multiple-choice items primarily for recognition, discrimination, application, and interpretation. Use true-false sparingly because guessing is high and statements easily become ambiguous. Use matching only for homogeneous sets of terms, names, events, or examples.

## 8. Constructed-response item extensions

Use constructed-response items when the target is explanation, comparison, justification, synthesis, or transfer. Distinguish:

- Restricted-response item: focused answer, narrower scoring, higher reliability.
- Extended-response item: broader integration, richer evidence, lower scoring reliability unless rubric is strong.

Scoring safeguards:

- Develop model answers and analytic scoring rubrics before administration.
- Specify required elements and acceptable alternatives.
- Score one question at a time across all papers when possible.
- Avoid letting handwriting, length, or irrelevant eloquence dominate scoring.

## 9. Performance assessment and portfolio extensions

Use performance tasks when the target is authentic application or production. A valid task needs:

- A realistic and course-aligned performance context.
- Clear task instructions and constraints.
- Explicit evaluation criteria.
- Procedures to reduce rater error.
- A record of evidence used for grading.

For portfolios, define purpose, required artifacts, selection rules, reflection requirements, scoring criteria, and revision policy.

## 10. Grading and reporting

Separate feedback from summative evaluation where feasible. When combining components:

- Use a transparent weighting rule.
- Ensure each component reflects the intended course outcomes.
- Avoid combining achievement with effort or improvement unless the grading policy explicitly includes those constructs.
- Communicate grading criteria before assessment.

## 11. Accommodations and fairness

Review whether item performance may be influenced by construct-irrelevant factors:

- Presentation format.
- Response format.
- Timing.
- Setting.
- Language demand.
- Cultural background knowledge.
- Disability-related access barriers.

Accommodations should preserve the target construct. For example, additional time may be appropriate when speed is not part of the construct, but not when speed is intentionally assessed.

## 12. Bias review

Bias review should not rely only on mean group differences. Examine:

- Inappropriate content or examples.
- Inappropriate language load.
- Examiner / administration effects.
- Differential familiarity with contexts.
- Whether the item may measure a different construct for different groups.
- Differential predictive or criterion relationships when data exist.

## 13. Best-practice workflow

Organize rigorous assessment work into five professional activities:

1. Developing assessments.
2. Selecting assessments.
3. Administering assessments.
4. Scoring assessments.
5. Interpreting, using, and communicating assessment results.

For agent behavior, this means the final output should include not only items, but also administration assumptions, scoring rules, interpretation cautions, and revision notes whenever relevant.
